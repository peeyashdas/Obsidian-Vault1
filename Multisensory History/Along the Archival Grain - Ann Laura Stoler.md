Along the Archival Grain: EPISTEMIC ANXIETIES AND COLONIAL COMMON SENSE  
  
Main Takeaway: Ann Laura Stoler's Along the Archival Grain challenges conventional approaches to colonial archives, advocating for an ethnographic reading that uncovers the "pulse" of these documents as sites of epistemological and political anxiety, rather than mere repositories of state power or biased sources. The book explores how colonial knowledge was produced, contested, and shaped by affective states, racial categories, and the very administrative practices meant to control information.

Chapter Two: The Pulse of the Archive  
  
This chapter serves as an introduction to Stoler's methodological approach to colonial archives, contrasting it with traditional historical readings.  
  
Pramoedya Ananta Toer's House of Glass  
  
Context: The novel (set in 1912 Dutch East Indies) explores the role of archives in colonial governance and suppression of nationalist movements.  
  
Protagonist: Jacques Pangemanann, a Eurasian former police officer, tasked to read classified state archives, spy on, and destroy nationalist leader Minke.  Archival Exclusion: Pangemanann's meticulous report, though praised by sugar industry leaders, is deemed too sensitive and its author too "lowly" (as an "Indo" or "mixed blood") to be officially preserved in the state archives. It is burned after reading.  
  
"Only a very few people in the Indies and in the world have read and studied it. . . . You will never know, and indeed do not need to know, who else has read it. Your work of scholarship, as you like to call it, will never receive the honor of being kept in the State archives. Once being read, it will become dust and smoke, in the safekeeping of the devils of darkness."  
  
"House of Glass" Metaphor: Refers to Pangemanann's report, but also symbolizes the fragile security of the Dutch police state and the false security of Europeans within it.  
  
Pramoedya's Critique: The novel condemns colonial rule and postcolonial power, targeting the "erudite, educated ignorance" cultivated by Dutch officialdom and contained within colonial archives. For Pramoedya, archives are "inert remnants" and a "mausoleum" that fails to capture the vibrant political culture of Java.  
  
Stoler's Approach to the Archive  
  
**Stoler argues against Pramoedya's view of a "dead" archive. Colonial state archives are dynamic "sites of perturbations," revealing "piecemeal partiality," "spasmodic and sustained currents of anxious labor," and epistemic anxieties.**  

**Focus**:  
  
***Stoler tries to elaborate on why and how the archives are dynamic as opposed to Pramoedya’s view?***  
  
Archiving-as-process rather than archives-as-things.  
  
Archives as "condensed sites of epistemological and political anxiety." *What is condensed site of epistemological and political anxiety?*  
  
 Examining the principles and practices of governance embedded in archival forms (prose style, repetitive refrains, persuasion, classification, genres of documentation).  
  
 **Content & Contributors**: Official Dutch colonial archives (late 19th and early 20th centuries) include not just bureaucratic reports but also contributions from non-salaried individuals (doctors, clergy, teachers) whose "local knowledge" shaped views on Dutch identity, concubinage, and "mixed blood" children.  
  
**Unconventional Discoveries**: Archives contain chronicles of failed projects, delusional imaginings, and expressions of distrust. They reveal the anxieties of Dutch administrators, German/French planters, and the struggles of impoverished Europeans.  
  
 **Developing Historical Negatives**" (Chapter 4): A strategy to track the fears, visions, and unrealized policies ("blueprints of distress") that stirred official anxiety and led to often "infeasible policies."  
  
 **Archives as a "Force Field**": Animates political energies, converts "social facts" into "qualified knowledge," and registers attractions and aversions operating within the colonial state's assertions of imperial rights.  
  
 "**Storeyed Archival Field Roland Bathes**”:  Documents are layered with practical deceptions and dispositions. Stoler focuses on the "hands and habits" of those creating and managing documents, including the "unremarkable forms" and the visibility/invisibility of different authors (e.g., Eurasian clerks as "copy machines").  
  
Archival Conventions  
  
• Cautionary Reading: Echoing Arlette Farge, Stoler emphasizes the need to approach archives as an "adversary," to "unlearn" initial assumptions about their meaning.  
  
• "Frail Conceit" of Panopticism: Official documents, despite fixed formats and clichés, often reflect assumed mastery rather than comprehensive knowledge. Administrative overviews were compiled from hurried and cluttered reports.  
  
• "Hierarchy of Credibility" (Chapter 6): Uncensored phrases, doubts, rumors, and contradictory testimonies reveal implicit scales of trust regarding witnesses and their accounts.  
  
• Inversion of Hierarchies: In moments of crisis (e.g., murder, impending attack), unauthorized or inappropriate words could gain significance, challenging official narratives. This includes "inside jobs" by civil servants (like Frans Carl Valck, chapters 6 & 7), recalcitrant Indo-Europeans (chapter 5), and protesting European officials (chapter 3).  
  
• Racialized Common Sense: Archives reveal implicit racial assumptions about various populations (Javanese coolies, Acehnese insurgents, Indische population, echte Europeans), especially when evidence was scarce.  
  
• Conventions as "Moving Targets": Irony and misinterpretations were present. "Stock phrases" shifted political import. "Information out of place" (e.g., European nurseries in classified documents on pauperism) highlights anxieties about subject-formation and the "psychic space of empire."  
  
### The Seductions of State Secrets  
  
• "Shadowed Places": Following Mary Douglas, Stoler examines how states create "shadowed places" through concealment (cryptic titles, nondescript bureaus, "lost" papers) as emblematic conventions of the archival form.  
  
• State Sovereignty: Resides in the power to designate arbitrary social facts (e.g., Indo children breastfed by native servants, poor whites with non-Christian names) as "matters of security and concerns of state."  
  
• Weber's "Official Secret": Bureaucracy's "fanatically defended" prize possession. Dutch Indies documents marked "secret" (X), "very secret," or "highly confidential" were elevated.  
  
• Purpose of Secrets: Not just to conceal, but to create categories and direct attention. They called upon intelligence technologies (secret police, informants like vertrouwensmannen or Eurasians).  
  
• Beyond Overt Secrets: Many "confidential" documents were not true secrets locally (e.g., European beggars in Batavia). The real "secret" often lay in the timing and interpretive uncertainties surrounding these facts, or disagreements among officials about racial classification (e.g., 1874 reports on "vagabond whites" were secret due to debate over numbers, 39 vs. thousands).  
  
• Secrets as "Sites of Unease": Anticipatory warnings of emergent movements, "structures of feeling," and underlying resentments. As Fredrick Barth noted, secrets invoke deeper secrets.  
  
• Critique and Disclosure: "Dirty secrets" (e.g., Sumatra planters' abuses) were classified not because unknown, but because they were not to be publicly acknowledged by "inept" civil servants like Frans Carl Valck.  
  
### Colonial Commissions  
  
• Archives as Technologies of State Reproduction: Building on Andrew Ashforth and Max Weber, Stoler views state bureaucracies, particularly their written accountability systems, as central to state power and reproduction.  
  
• Role of Commissions of Inquiry: State-sponsored commissions reorganized knowledge, reconstructed historical narratives, and often responded to or even generated crises. They could reactivate or halt knowledge, acting as "technologies of delay."  
  
• "Producers of Social Kinds" (Chapter 5): Commissions (e.g., European Pauperism Commission of 1901) created new categories of people and problems, revising racial ascriptions. They used statistics, historical narrative, and anecdotes to "identify distinct sorts of persons, with specific dispositions and states of mind."  
  
• Tools of Statecraft: Like statistics, commissions were 19th-century tools for social reform, measuring morality and unfreedom.  
  
• Politics of Comparison: Commissions leveraged biopolitical assessments of racial capabilities, comparing "mixed blood" or "poor white" problems across the imperial globe (e.g., South Africa, Australia).  
  
• Domestic to State Security: They explicitly linked domestic relationships (parent-child, nursemaid-infant) and even relationships to objects (clothing, furnishings) to state security, using intimate testimonies as critical data.  
  
• "Quasi-State" Technologies: Commissions often involved "outside" experts, lending objectivity and verifying the state's right to assess public interest, thus demonstrating state power through its "will to the production of truth."  
  
### Ethnography in the Archives  
  
• Rethinking Fieldwork: Stoler argues that archival productions should be treated as ethnography, similar to how Sally Falk-Moore advocated treating fieldwork as "current history."  
  
• "Immersement": Following Marilyn Strathern, Stoler seeks to construct a mode of inquiry that returns to existing fields of knowledge "in the hindsight of unpredicted outcomes," recovering material investigators were not aware they were collecting.  
  
• Ethnographic Space: Found in the "disjuncture between prescription and practice," state mandates and people's maneuvers, normative rules and lived lives.  
  
• Process, Not Repository: Archives are not just repositories of state power but "unquiet movements in a field of force," sites of "rituals of possession," "relics and ruins," and "contested cultural knowledge." Sentiments are treated as judgments and tracers of power.  
  
• Beyond "Sources" of Truth: The focus shifts from distinguishing fact from fiction to tracking the production and consumption of "facticities" as contingent coordinates of specific times and purposes. This exposes how filing systems and disciplined writing create control.  
  
• "Arrested Histories": New studies tackle the politics of colonial knowledge and the "histories suspended from received historiography" that result.  
  
• Broadened Scope: Ethnographic sensibilities lead to examining oral/vernacular histories, unacknowledged native expertise, diverse genres of documentation, and "collages of memory" that provide new forms of historical evidence.  
  
• "Teaching Task": The Latin root of "document" (docere) implies a teaching task. Stoler examines what and who was being educated by bureaucratic formulas.  
  
### Colonial Common Sense and Its Epistemic Frames  
  
• Foucault's Archive: An archive reveals "the rules of practice" and "the discontinuity that separate us from what we can no longer say."  
  
• "Social Etymologies": Stoler traces the careers of words and categories (e.g., "pauper" and "white"), identifying the political practices and power relationships buried within them. This shows how "social kinds" were produced.  
  
• Categorical Shifts: New categories gained relevance by annulling old designations (e.g., the contested term "Inlandsche hinderen" in chapter 4, referring to mixed-background or illegitimate Europeans in the late 19th century, which then declined by the 1920s). These discrepancies are "diacritics of the patent and latent distinctions" in colonial epistemology, marking Foucault's "breach of the self-evident."  
  
• "Habit-Taking" Processes: Drawing on C.S. Peirce, Stoler investigates how people align with existing forces, forming "epistemic habits" and "grids of intelligibility" that make certain conventions seem obvious or strange.  
  
• Common Sense as Political: Common sense is historical and political; dispositions are trained, disciplined, and often contested, shaping colonial governance.  
  
• "Epistemic Habits": Historically steeped ways of knowing that are "easy to think," temporary, but challengeable.  
  
• Epistemic Uncertainty: Rather than clarity, epistemic uncertainty often generated the densest debates and extensive paper trails in colonial archives, revealing that colonial truth-claims were provisional.  
  
Affective Strains  
  
• "Habits of a Colonial Heart" (Chapter 3): Explores the interplay between reason and sentiment, which was crucial for political assessments but often escaped bureaucratic "calculation" (Weber).  
  
• "Para-Ethnography": Douglas Holmes' term for the everyday queries and details that had to be "sensed" but not measured by enumeration.  
  
• Managed Hearts: Colonial projects demanded specific sentiments (attachment, pity, contempt) and assessed racial membership based on who expressed/received these feelings.  
  
• Sentiments as Judgments: Not opposed to reason, sentiments are seen as "judgments, assessments, and interpretations of the social and political world," serving as markers of rank and exemption.  
  
• Archival Role in Emotional Economy: Seen in the measured tone of texts, biting marginalia, and moral assessments in footnotes.  
  
• Epistemological Virtue: Inquiry into who and what was granted epistemological virtue based on cultural competencies and social criteria (Steven Shapin).  
  
• Administrative Anxiety: Focused on ungauged affective states of European colonials (e.g., the 1848 Batavia demonstration where family attachments clashed with state loyalty), highlighting the "contagious, transient quality of sentiment."  
  
• "Taming of Chance": Both scientific communities and colonial governance were preoccupied with measuring uncertainties and allowing for prediction (Jan Hacking).  
  
• Epistemic Warrant & Power: Granting epistemic warrant is a "covert way of distributing power." Stoler critiques over-reliance on Foucault's "episteme" or "regime of truth" for their connotations of too much stability, preferring "epistemic habits" to capture competing investments and shifting claims.  
  
### Tracing the Archival Turn  
  
• "Archive-as-Subject": The shift from viewing archives as mere sources to understanding them as subjects of inquiry, an "agentive act" focused on the politics of knowledge.  
  
• Blurring Boundaries: The traditional distinction between "the archive" for historians (documents) and "the Archive" for cultural theorists (metaphorical collections) is increasingly blurred.  
  
• "Ethnography in an Archival Mode": While historians like Carlo Ginzburg and Natalie Davis used "hostile" documents to reveal hidden truths in the archive, Stoler calls for ethnography of the archive itself, examining its production and mechanisms.  
  
• Archivists' Contributions: Archivists themselves increasingly engage with the politics and history of archives, questioning what information matters, how tacit narratives inform practices, and the impact of changing storage technologies.  
  
### Colonialism's Archival Grains  
  
• "Against the Grain" vs. "Along the Grain": While many scholars read colonial archives "against their grain" to uncover subaltern agency and critique official knowledge (Ranajit Guha), Stoler argues for first reading "along the grain" with care. This means examining the assumptions embedded in colonial statecraft, which often prioritized selective winnowing and reduction of knowledge rather than mere accumulation.  
  
• "Ethnographic Exercise": Stoler proposes moving from an "extractive exercise" to an "ethnographic one" when engaging with archives.  
  
• "Pulse of the Archive": Stoler's own experience with reports of a 1876 Sumatra murder highlighted the startling contrasts in documents—neat vs. hurried, fine-grained knowledge vs. ignorance—revealing the complex workings of empire.  
  
• Archives as Sites of Command and Countermand: Colonial archives were "a corpus of statements and a depot of documents," "sites of the imaginary and institutions that fashioned histories." Power and control are inherent in the etymology of "archive" (Greek arkhe, "to command").  
  
• Reading Process: Documents were made important by their appearance, cross-referencing, and cataloguing, which created "paths of precedent" and mapped relevance.  
  
• Muted Events: Instead of focusing solely on dramatic "reversals of a relationship of forces" (Foucault), Stoler looks for "archival events" that "disrupt (if only provisionally) a field of force," challenge "epistemic warrant," and realign probabilities in more subtle ways.  
  
• "Watermarks of Empire": The final chapters adopt a "different register," exploring lives that slip in and out of official archives.  
  
• Chapter 7, "Imperial Dispositions of Disregard": Examines the "politics of disregard"—the psychological and political mechanisms required to ignore uncomfortable truths within empire.  
  
• Frans Carl Valck: His story is traced through both government archives (Ch. 6) and a private family archive (Ch. 7), revealing a "history of empire 'in a minor key'" by connecting his personal and public lives, and their confused sensibilities.  
  
• Conclusion: Stoler's work focuses on the "fault lines, ragged edges, and unremarked disruptions" within seemingly seamless archival genres. It emphasizes the "granular rather than seamless texture" of the archive, revealing "how unintelligibilities are sustained and why empires remain so uneasily invested in them." This is "history in the ethnographic grain" (Robert Darnton), analyzing how people thought about how they thought.
