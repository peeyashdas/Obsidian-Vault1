In [[Along the Archival Grain - Ann Laura Stoler]] , historian Ann Laura Stoler challenges the traditional view that colonial archives are monoliths of absolute, supreme power. Instead, she argues that if you look closely at the margins, the edits, and the frantic categorization within colonial records, you see a paper trail of epistemic anxiety  
  
"Epistemic" relates to knowledge—how we know what we know, and what we accept as truth. For colonial authorities, keeping power wasn't just about military force; it was about maintaining a strict monopoly on truth and rationality. Colonial rule was justified by a specific hierarchy: the European was rational, scientific, and orderly; the colonized subject was superstitious, emotional, and disordered.  
  
**Epistemic anxiety occurs when colonial authorities encounter something they cannot classify, predict, or explain using their Western, rational categories.** It is the creeping panic that the colonial system of knowledge might actually be inadequate or fragile. To manage this anxiety, officials would over-produce reports, build rigid taxonomies, and obsessively document the phenomenon to force it back into a box where they felt in control.  
  
When we apply Stoler's concept to C. Yamini Krishna’s analysis of Edmund Henderson Hunt’s 1928 amateur film _Rafai Fakirs_, the camera ceases to be just a recording device. Instead, it becomes a literal shield against epistemic anxiety.  
  
### Applying Epistemic Anxiety to _Rafai Fakirs_  
  
We can trace exactly how Hunt’s film acts as a response to colonial anxiety across three distinct layers:  
  
#### 1. The Challenge to Medical Rationality  
  
As the Chief Medical Officer of the Nizam State Railway, Hunt’s entire worldview was built on Western medical science, physiology, and anatomy. When he witnessed the Rafai Fakirs performing severe acts of self-mutilation (like driving spikes into their eyes or cutting their flesh) _without showing typical signs of shock, infection, or fatal trauma_, it struck at the heart of his medical certainty.  
  
According to Western medical logic, these bodies should have broken. The fact that they didn't created a profound epistemic crisis. Hunt couldn't simply ignore it; his medical authority was threatened by a reality he could not explain.  
  
#### 2. The Camera as a Tool of Containment  
  
To manage this anxiety, Hunt turned to the camera. By filming the Fakirs, Hunt attempted to convert an unsettling, borderline inexplicable spiritual phenomenon into a series of static, empirical data points.  
  
- **Framing and Isolation:** The camera allows the colonial observer to zoom in, slow down, and isolate specific body parts. By focusing intensely on the wounds, the spikes, and the skin, Hunt attempted to strip the act of its religious, mystical, and anti-colonial meaning.  
     
- **The Illusion of Objectivity:** The camera provided an illusion of distance and control. If it could be captured on a strip of film, it could be brought back to a laboratory, shown to peers, and subjected to standard scientific scrutiny. Filming was an attempt to domesticate a practice that felt dangerously wild and unruly.  
     
  
#### 3. The Over-Documentation Symptom  
  
A classic sign of Stoler's epistemic anxiety is the frantic need to over-document and over-explain. Hunt didn't just take a quick snapshot; he created a meticulous, four-part film structure accompanied by detailed notes.  
  
Yet, as Krishna points out, the anxiety remains visible in the margins of the archive. The very existence of this elaborate visual record testifies to the fact that Western science _failed_ to completely explain the Fakirs away. No matter how much Hunt slowed down the footage or analyzed the flesh, the underlying mechanism of their endurance eluded his medical taxonomy. The film stands as a permanent archive of a colonial official trying—and ultimately failing—to achieve total intellectual mastery over the colonized body.  
  
### The Archival Paradox  
  
By reading Hunt’s project through Stoler, we see a fascinating paradox that also links back to Neepa Majumdar's ideas on the fragment. Hunt made the film to assert control and prove his scientific capability. But today, the surviving fragment reveals the exact opposite to modern historians: ==it exposes a moment where the colonial apparatus felt profoundly unsettled, anxious, and deeply aware of the limits of its own knowledge.==  
  
Neepa Majumdar and Yamini Krishna  
  
In her analysis of Edmund Henderson Hunt’s 1928 footage, C. Yamini Krishna demonstrates that ==the archival encounter was far from a one-sided act of colonial extraction.== While Hunt viewed his camera as an objective, empirical instrument designed to capture and master the "irrational" practices of the Rafai Fakirs, the Fakirs themselves exercised significant agency.  
  
They did not merely submit to his medical gaze; instead, they **subverted, co-opted, and renegotiated** the filming process, exploiting the limitations of Hunt’s Western scientific lens in three primary ways:  
  
### 1. Reclaiming the Performance on Their Own Terms  
  
In any ethnographic or scientific filming encounter, there is a struggle over _what_ is being recorded and _why_. Hunt wanted a cold, clinical, physiological record of bodily trauma to dissect under a Western medical taxonomy. The Fakirs, however, maintained the integrity of their spiritual practice.  
  
They did not perform isolated anatomical acts for a doctor; they performed a collective, rhythmically driven ritual of _dhikr_ (remembrance) and spiritual devotion. By refusing to strip away the communal, auditory, and ecstatic elements of their ritual, they forced Hunt to film an unbroken spiritual tradition. Their bodies remained anchored to their own sacred cosmology, completely defying Hunt's attempts to reduce them to passive clinical specimens.  
  
### 2. The Agency of Consent and Collaboration  
  
Krishna’s reading of the archive exposes a fundamental reality of early "amateur" filmmaking: **Hunt could not have made this film without the active compliance, coordination, and choices of the Fakirs.**  
  
The acts being recorded—such as driving spikes near the eyes or piercing the flesh—required immense focus, specialized skill, and specific environmental conditions. The Fakirs dictated the pacing, the boundaries, and the execution of these acts. By controlling the physical reality of what the camera was allowed to see, they turned the filming space into a negotiation. Hunt was entirely dependent on their willingness to perform, shifting the power dynamic from a colonial authority commanding a subject to a filmmaker reliant on an expert practitioner.  
  
### 3. Exposing the Limits of the "Medical Gaze"  
  
The ultimate subversion occurred through the physical reality of the Fakirs' bodies. Hunt deployed the camera to demystify what he viewed as "tricks" or primitive anomalies. He used slow-motion, tight close-ups, and structural framing to isolate wounds, searching for an anatomical trick or a physical explanation that would satisfy Western medicine.  
  
The Fakirs' bodies resisted this reduction. They bled minimally, showed no signs of standard physiological shock, and walked away without the infections or fatal trauma that Hunt’s medical training dictated should happen. By successfully executing these profound physical feats right in front of his lens, the Fakirs weaponized the camera against Hunt's own worldview. Instead of proving the supremacy of Western science, the footage became an irrefutable record of something Western science could not explain, leaving behind an archive of colonial confusion rather than absolute mastery.
